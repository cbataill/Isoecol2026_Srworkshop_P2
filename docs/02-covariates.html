2. Covariates

Geology, climate, atmospheric deposition, soils and vegetation

Load ~60 rasters and compute neighbourhood geology
Modified

October 9, 2026

In this section
  • You’ll learn: which covariates can predict bioavailable ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr, and why
  • You’ll need: Project_Raster/clipped_za/
  • Time: about 20 minutes

Overview

We now load the covariates used to train the random forest. They describe the geological, environmental and climatic processes that control bioavailable ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr: the sources of Sr (bedrock, dust, sea salt), how it is mixed and weathered (soils, climate, sediments) and how it is taken up (vegetation).

Same projection, same grid

The rasters must share the same projection and resolution, and be snapped to each other. This preprocessing is slow for global layers, so it has been done for you: the global Eckert IV 1 km rasters (OSF) are already clipped to South Africa in Project_Raster/clipped_za.

Want to build an isoscape for your own study area? See Applying this to your own study area at the end of this page.

The full list of global covariates

Compared with the global script, a few global layers are not in the South Africa set (Bouguer anomaly, permafrost, mean annual temperature and the other WorldClim variables except wind). Permafrost is irrelevant in South Africa anyway!

Step 2.8: Neighbourhood geology (NEW)

The bedrock ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr model (r.srsrq1) and the age of the geological units (r.meanage_geol) describe the rock directly under each 1 km pixel, with sharp boundaries between units. But bioavailable Sr at a site does not come only from the rock under it:

  • soils form on sediments, colluvium and alluvium transported from the surrounding hills;
  • dust and runoff mix material from the surrounding area;
  • plants and animals integrate Sr over their root zone or home range;
  • the sample coordinates and the geological contacts both have location errors of hundreds of metres to kilometres.

To represent this, we compute the mean of the bedrock ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr and of the geological age within circles of 5, 10 and 25 km around each pixel (a moving-window or “focal” mean). The random forest can then learn at which scale the geology best predicts bioavailable ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr.

focal_dir <- "Project_Raster/neighbourhood"
dir.create(focal_dir, showWarnings = FALSE)

focal_names <- c()
for (v in c("r.srsrq1", "r.meanage_geol")) {
  for (km in c(5, 10, 25)) {
    name <- sprintf("%s.f%02d", v, km)               # e.g. r.srsrq1.f05 = mean within 5 km
    file <- file.path(focal_dir, paste0(name, ".tif"))
    if (!file.exists(file)) {
      r <- get(v)
      w <- focalMat(r, km * 1000, type = "circle")   # circular window, radius in metres
      w[w > 0] <- 1                                   # simple (unweighted) mean
      f <- focal(r, w = w, fun = "mean", na.rm = TRUE)
      f <- mask(f, r)                                 # keep the land mask of the original raster
      writeRaster(f, file, overwrite = TRUE)
    }
    assign(name, terra::rast(file))
    focal_names <- c(focal_names, name)
  }
}
focal_names
plot(c(r.srsrq1, r.srsrq1.f05, r.srsrq1.f10, r.srsrq1.f25), nc = 4)
Why stop at 25 km?

In the training data, adding these six layers improves the spatial cross-validation R² from ~0.5 to ~0.7. Going beyond 25 km does not help: at 50 km the layers become so smooth that they behave like coordinates.

Things you might have noticed

🧭 Only in this South African model

  • The 1:1M geological map (Council for Geoscience): r.geomap
  • iSDA Africa soil chemistry: extractable Ca, Mg, K, Al, Fe, P, S, Zn, eCEC and texture at 0–20 and 20–50 cm
  • The SANBI vegetation map (Mucina & Rutherford 2006): r.vegmap
  • Neighbourhood geology: mean bedrock ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr and age within 5, 10 and 25 km. Spatial CV R² goes from ~0.5 to ~0.7

🔬 Link each one to a process

  • Extractable Ca, Mg: the exchangeable pool plants draw from; carbonate, calcrete and shell input
  • Al, Fe: weathering intensity. Texture: transport and mixing of sediments
  • Vegetation: climate, rooting depth, biome

⚠️ Not every new layer helps

  • Categorical maps with hundreds of classes: most classes contain no training site, so they are dropped in Step 3.5.
  • The coarse aerosol models (~1–2°) behave like coordinates inside one country.
  • r.africa.Ca.0 made it into the final model; the categorical maps did not.

🧰 For your own isoscape

  • Choose by process (source × mixing × uptake), not by availability.
  • Match the resolution to your sampling scale, and cover the whole prediction area.
  • Look for regional data: detailed geological maps, airborne radiometric surveys (K, Th, U), soil geochemistry, loess and till maps.

Take-home: a regional covariate is worth adding only if it encodes a Sr process and your training sites sample its range.

Applying this to your own study area

The rasters in Project_Raster/clipped_za are the global covariates, already clipped to South Africa. To build an isoscape somewhere else, go back to the global rasters and clip them to your own area. The rest of the script stays the same.

1. Download the global rasters

Download the global covariates (Eckert IV, 1 km) from the Projected_rasters folder of the OSF project, and put them all in one folder, e.g. Global_rasters/ next to Data/ and Project_Raster/. They are already in the same projection and resolution and snapped to the same grid, so all you need to do is clip them.

Large files

The global 1 km rasters are much larger than the South African ones. Download only the layers you need, and keep enough free disk space for the clipped copies.

2. Clip them to your study area

Define your study area as a polygon, reproject it to Eckert IV, add a buffer, then crop and mask every raster.

library(terra)
library(sf)
library(rnaturalearth)

eck4 <- "+proj=eck4 +lon_0=0 +x_0=0 +y_0=0 +ellps=WGS84 +units=m +no_defs"

global_dir <- "Global_rasters"                  # folder with the global OSF rasters
out_dir    <- "Project_Raster/clipped_myarea"   # where the clipped rasters will go
dir.create(out_dir, recursive = TRUE, showWarnings = FALSE)

# Study area: here one or more countries from Natural Earth...
area <- ne_countries(country = c("Kenya", "Tanzania"), scale = "medium", returnclass = "sf")
# ...or your own polygon: area <- st_read("my_study_area.shp")

area <- area |>
  st_transform(eck4) |>
  st_union() |>
  st_buffer(50000)   # 50 km buffer (see the tip below)
area_v <- vect(area)

# Crop and mask every global raster to the study area
for (f in list.files(global_dir, pattern = "\\.tif$", full.names = TRUE)) {
  r <- rast(f)
  r <- crop(r, project(area_v, crs(r)), mask = TRUE)
  writeRaster(r, file.path(out_dir, basename(f)), overwrite = TRUE)
}
list.files(out_dir)
Why a buffer?

The neighbourhood covariates (Step 2.8) average the geology within 25 km. Near the edge of a tightly clipped area, those windows are cut in half. A buffer of at least 25 km (here 50 km) keeps them complete. It also lets you use training sites just outside your area of interest.

3. Point the script to your rasters

In the setup chunk, change the raster folder:

raster_dir <- "Project_Raster/clipped_myarea"

Then delete or rename Project_Raster/neighbourhood/, so that the neighbourhood layers (Step 2.8) are recomputed for your area instead of re-using the South African ones.

4. Adjust the list of covariates

Steps 2.2 to 2.7 load each raster by name, and Step 2.9 lists them in predictor_names. Every name in that list must exist in your folder.

  • Remove the South African layers: r.geomap and r.vegmap exist only for South Africa. The iSDA soil layers (r.africa.*) cover Africa only: outside Africa, remove them too.
  • Check the other layers: a few layers in the South African set are not part of the global script (r.pet_clim, r.pfert, r.biofuels, r.biogenic). Keep them only if you have them.
  • Add your own regional layers if they exist (detailed geological maps, radiometric surveys, soil geochemistry…). They must be in Eckert IV at 1 km, on the same grid. project(my_layer, r.srsrq1) does this for you.

Instead of editing Steps 2.2 to 2.7 one line at a time, you can load everything in the folder at once. Run this chunk in place of Steps 2.2 to 2.7:

files <- list.files(raster_dir, pattern = "\\.tif$", full.names = TRUE)
for (f in files) assign(sub("\\.tif$", "", basename(f)), rast(f))

Then run Step 2.8 (neighbourhood geology) as usual, and replace the predictor_names list in Step 2.9 with:

# Every raster in the folder is a candidate covariate, plus the neighbourhood layers of Step 2.8
predictor_names <- c(sub("\\.tif$", "", basename(files)), focal_names)

In the rest of Step 2.9, two lines refer to South African layers by name. Edit them if those layers are not in your folder:

  • for (p in c("r.geomap", "r.vegmap")) levels(stack_all[[p]]) <- NULL: delete it if you have no categorical maps (or put your own categorical layers in the list).
  • plot(stack_all[[c(..., "r.africa.Ca.0")]]): remove "r.africa.Ca.0" outside Africa.

5. Use your own training data

Replace Data/SouthAfrica_bioavailable_Sr_ARDUOUS.csv with screened bioavailable ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr data for your area, in the ARDUOUS format (see Module 1 and the ARDUOUS dataset). The script uses the columns collection_decimal_longitude, collection_decimal_latitude, 87Sr86Sr and material_type.

6. Re-think a few choices

  • Coarse layers (Step 3.5): inside one country, dust and sea-salt models are smooth gradients that act like coordinates. Over a continent, they vary for real and can be useful. Compare "all" and "no_coarse" with the spatial cross-validation.
  • Spatial folds (Step 4.2): choose the number of clusters to match your sampling design, and check that the cross-validation distances look like the map’s.
  • Time and memory: a larger area means more pixels. If the prediction (Step 5.2) runs out of memory, increase steps.
  • Few data in your area? A locally trained model needs enough sites to learn from. With very few data, a globally trained isoscape (Bataille et al. 2020) is often the better starting point.

Summary

Key takeaways
  • Covariates describe the sources, mixing and uptake of Sr.
  • Regional datasets (soil chemistry, detailed geology) can add real information, but only if the training sites cover them.
  • Bioavailable Sr integrates more than the rock under the pixel: neighbourhood geology captures this.
  • To work in another area, clip the global OSF rasters to your study area (with a buffer) and adjust the covariate list.
